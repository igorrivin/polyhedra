17 Unfolding and the problem of finding constant series

17.1 What a volume computation determines

The problem is to integrate a sum over curves while the underlying surface also varies. Directly, this asks us to keep track of all the curves on every surface in moduli space. Unfolding changes the order of description: we integrate over surfaces equipped with one chosen curve, and evaluate just the term belonging to that curve. The sum disappears because the choice of curve has become part of the integration domain. An elementary example shows exactly how this trade works.

A toy model: unfolding the circle to the line.

Let f:ℝ→[0,∞)f:\mathbb R\to[0,\infty) be integrable, and form the periodic sum Pf(x)=∑n∈ℤf(x+n).P_f(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}f(x+n). Shifting xx by one merely reindexes the sum, so PfP_f is a function on the circle ℝ/ℤ\mathbb R/\mathbb Z. A fundamental interval for that circle is [0,1)[0,1): each point of the circle has one representative there. By nonnegativity, Tonelli’s theorem allows us to interchange sum and integral: ∫01Pf(x)⁡dx=∑n∈ℤ∫01f(x+n)⁡dx=∑n∈ℤ∫nn+1f(u)⁡du=∫ℝf(u)⁡du.\begin{aligned} \int_0^1P_f(x)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm dx &=\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}\int_0^1f(x+n)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm dx\\ &=\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}\int_n^{n+1}f(u)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm du =\int_{\mathbb R}f(u)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm du. \end{aligned} In the second line we used u=x+nu=x+n. The translated intervals [n,n+1)[n,n+1) tile the real line, so they join into a single integration domain. This is unfolding: one integral of a sum on a quotient becomes one integral of a single function on a larger space. For a signed or complex-valued ff, the same argument works if ∫ℝ|f|<∞\int_{\mathbb R}|f|<\infty.

Take f(u)=e−|u|f(u)=e^{-|u|}. For 0≤x≤10\le x\le1, separate n≥0n\ge0 from n≤−1n\le-1 and sum the two geometric series: Pf(x)=e−x+ex−11−e−1,∫01Pf(x)⁡dx=2.P_f(x)=\frac{e^{-x}+e^{x-1}}{1-e^{-1}}, \qquad \int_0^1P_f(x)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm dx=2. Knowing the integral has not determined the function: Pf(0)=1+e−11−e−1,Pf(12)=2e−1/21−e−1,P_f(0)=\frac{1+e^{-1}}{1-e^{-1}},\qquad P_f(\tfrac12)=\frac{2e^{-1/2}}{1-e^{-1}}, and these values differ by (1−e−1/2)2/(1−e−1)>0(1-e^{-1/2})^2/(1-e^{-1})>0. Thus unfolding computes the mean, here 22, without making the periodic sum constant.

A contrasting example is the tent function f(u)=max⁡(1−|u|,0)f(u)=\max(1-|u|,0). For 0≤x≤10\le x\le1 only the terms with n=0,−1n=0,-1 can be nonzero, and their sum is (1−x)+x=1(1-x)+x=1. Its translates form a partition of unity: their values add to one at every point. This extra pointwise fact explains constancy; integrating it also gives the value of the integral. McShane’s identity will supply a geometric partition playing a comparable role.

What replaces the circle and the line?

Fix an oriented surface and its boundary lengths. Teichmüller space 𝒯\mathcal T consists of its marked hyperbolic structures: the marking identifies curves with curves on a fixed reference surface. The mapping class group Γ\Gamma consists of orientation-preserving diffeomorphisms modulo isotopy, with the prescribed boundary conventions. Its action changes the marking. The quotient Γ∖𝒯\Gamma\backslash\mathcal T is moduli space, where the marking has been forgotten.

Choose an essential, nonperipheral curve class γ\gamma: it cannot be contracted to a point or moved into a puncture or boundary. Its orbit Γγ\Gamma\gamma consists of all classes obtained by changing the marking, each counted once. Write ℓδ(X)\ell_\delta(X) for the length of the geodesic representative of δ\delta on XX. The subgroup Stab⁡(γ)\mathop{\mathrm{Stab}}(\gamma) preserves γ\gamma. Consequently Stab⁡(γ)∖𝒯\mathop{\mathrm{Stab}}(\gamma)\backslash\mathcal T remembers a surface together with a distinguished curve in this orbit. The map to Γ∖𝒯\Gamma\backslash\mathcal T forgets that curve. The different choices over a surface are precisely what the orbit sum counts, with automorphisms handled by orbifold integration. Curves of equal length are still distinct summands when their classes are different.

Use the same Weil–Petersson (WP) orbifold measure ⁡dVWP\mathop{}\!\mathrm dV_{\mathrm{WP}} on both quotients. For a measurable nonnegative function FF, or one for which ∫Γ∖𝒯∑δ|F(ℓδ)|⁡dVWP<∞\int_{\Gamma\backslash\mathcal T}\sum_\delta|F(\ell_\delta)|\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm dV_{\mathrm{WP}}<\infty, the basic unfolding identity is ∫Γ∖𝒯∑δ∈ΓγF(ℓδ(X))⁡dVWP(X)=∫Stab⁡(γ)∖𝒯F(ℓγ(X))⁡dVWP(X).\int_{\Gamma\backslash\mathcal T} \sum_{\delta\in\Gamma\gamma}F(\ell_\delta(X))\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm dV_{\mathrm{WP}}(X) =\int_{\mathop{\mathrm{Stab}}(\gamma)\backslash\mathcal T} F(\ell_\gamma(X))\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm dV_{\mathrm{WP}}(X).(17.1) To prove it, take a fundamental domain for Γ\Gamma and integrate each summand over that domain. Change the marking in each term so that the chosen curve becomes γ\gamma. The resulting translates assemble into a fundamental domain for Stab⁡(γ)\mathop{\mathrm{Stab}}(\gamma), just as the translated unit intervals assembled into ℝ\mathbb R. The stabilizer identifies exactly the repetitions that would otherwise count the same chosen curve again. Ineffective isotropy is retained on both sides: even symmetries that act trivially contribute the reciprocal of their group order. For nonnegative functions either integral may be infinite.

The same argument applies to a specified multicurve, with Stab⁡(γ)\mathop{\mathrm{Stab}}(\gamma) preserving its entire decoration, including any orientation, ordering or component weights, and with the same length statistic on both sides. Simplicity is not needed for this change of integration domain.

Why remembering a simple curve helps.

If the chosen curve is simple, meaning it has no self-intersections, cutting along it produces two geodesic boundary circles of the same length xx. To reconstruct the surface we must specify the cut surface and how far one boundary is shifted before regluing it to the other. That shift is the Fenchel–Nielsen twist τ\tau, measured in length units. A full Dehn twist changes τ\tau to τ+x\tau+x, so a fundamental twist interval is 0≤τ<x0\le\tau<x. The WP length–twist factor is ⁡dx⁡dτ\mathop{}\!\mathrm dx\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm d\tau; integrating out the twist therefore gives ∫0x⁡dτ⁡dx=x⁡dx.\int_0^x\mathop{}\!\mathrm d\tau\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm dx=x\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm dx. This is the origin of the length factor in the unfolded integral. It is the size of the gluing parameter, rather than an extra weight chosen for the curve.

For a disjoint simple multicurve, repeat this construction along each component. The result has factors ∏jxj⁡dxj\prod_j x_j\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm dx_j and the WP volumes of the cut components. For example, cutting a nonseparating curve on a genus-gg surface with nn boundaries leaves a genus-(g−1)(g-1) surface with n+2n+2 boundaries, the two new lengths both being xx. A separating cut leaves two surfaces, so their volume factors multiply. Finite symmetries must still be accounted for: unoriented cuffs or indistinguishable components must not be accidentally counted twice. Mirzakhani’s integration formula makes these factors precise [75, Theorem 7.1 and Lemma 7.3]. Its symmetry and one-handle factors must be converted using the volume convention for each component; they are not extra factors to append to an already stack-normalized quotient.

An integral is an average; an identity needs more.

Suppose a series S(X)S(X) is independently known to be constant, say CC, and 0<Vg,n<∞0<V_{g,n}<\infty in the chosen normalization. Then C=1Vg,n∫ℳg,nS(X)⁡dVWP(X),C=\frac{1}{V_{g,n}} \int_{\mathcal M_{g,n}}S(X)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm dV_{\mathrm{WP}}(X),(17.2) and unfolding can calculate its value. Without an independent constancy argument the same quotient is only the mean of SS, as the exponential toy example shows. Conversely, if a pointwise identity supplies a known nonzero CC, evaluating its integral can determine the unknown volume Vg,nV_{g,n}. This second use is the starting point of Mirzakhani’s volume recursion.

A complete surface example: the punctured torus.

Let XX be a complete finite-area hyperbolic torus with one cusp. All essential unoriented simple closed curves belong to a single mapping class group orbit. Cutting along a chosen one, of length xx, leaves a pair of pants with boundary lengths (0,x,x)(0,x,x), where length zero denotes the cusp. A hyperbolic pair of pants is determined by its three boundary lengths. There is therefore no further moduli integral for the cut surface: the remaining continuous parameters are just x>0x>0 and the regluing twist τ\tau.

The effective stabilizer quotient has coordinates x>0x>0 and 0≤τ<x0\le\tau<x. In the stack convention of Section 6, the torus’s generic elliptic involution has order two and acts trivially on Teichmüller space. Orbifold integration retains its weight 1/21/2. Thus the unfolded integral is 12∫0∞∫0xf(x)⁡dτ⁡dx\frac12\int_0^\infty\int_0^x f(x)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm d\tau\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm dx, giving ∫ℳ1,1∑α simplef(ℓα)⁡dVWP=12∫0∞xf(x)⁡dx.\int_{\mathcal M_{1,1}}\sum_{\alpha\text{ simple}}f(\ell_\alpha)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm dV_{\mathrm{WP}} =\frac12\int_0^\infty x f(x)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm dx.(17.3) The sum is over unoriented, nonperipheral simple curve classes. The factor 1/21/2 occurs once; it is not a second quotient by cuff exchange.

Choose f(x)=(1+ex)−1f(x)=(1+e^x)^{-1}. The many-curve integral has become an elementary one-variable integral: ∫0∞x⁡dx1+ex=∑m≥1(−1)m−1∫0∞xe−mx⁡dx=∑m≥1(−1)m−1m2=π212.\int_0^\infty\frac{x\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm dx}{1+e^x} =\sum_{m\ge1}(-1)^{m-1}\int_0^\infty xe^{-mx}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm dx =\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{(-1)^{m-1}}{m^2} =\frac{\pi^2}{12}. Here (1+ex)−1=∑m≥1(−1)m−1e−mx(1+e^x)^{-1}=\sum_{m\ge1}(-1)^{m-1}e^{-mx} for x>0x>0. Termwise integration is justified by ∑m∫0∞xe−mx⁡dx=∑mm−2<∞\sum_m\int_0^\infty xe^{-mx}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm dx=\sum_m m^{-2}<\infty. For a finite numerical check, the integral beyond RR is at most (R+1)e−R(R+1)e^{-R}, and the alternating sum through m=Mm=M has error at most (M+1)−2(M+1)^{-2}. Equation (17.3) therefore gives π2/24\pi^2/24 for the integral over moduli space.

Why choose this particular ff? Because McShane’s theorem says that its curve sum is pointwise constant [73]: ∑α simple, unoriented11+eℓα(X)=12.\sum_{\alpha\text{ simple, unoriented}} \frac{1}{1+e^{\ell_\alpha(X)}}=\frac12.(17.4) The geometric idea is a partition at the cusp on a single fixed surface. Take a small horocycle around the cusp and normalize its length measure to have total mass one. Its points specify geodesic rays leaving the cusp. The gap construction groups the complementary intervals by simple curves; the exceptional set has measure zero. For a curve of length xx, the associated gaps have combined normalized measure 2/(1+ex)2/(1+e^x). This width is computed in the pair of pants obtained by cutting along that curve. Adding the gap widths gives one, hence (17.4). This describes the mechanism, not a proof of the required gap classification or null-set theorem; see [74, Sections 3, 5, and 9]. As with the tent function, the partition supplies the pointwise statement that integration alone cannot supply.

We can now use the two ingredients in either direction. Integrating McShane’s identity and using the computed integral gives 12V1,1=π224,V1,1=π212.\frac12 V_{1,1}=\frac{\pi^2}{24}, \qquad V_{1,1}=\frac{\pi^2}{12}. Alternatively, if the volume is already known from the tetrahedral calculation and torus–sphere correspondence, unfolding checks that the mean of the McShane sum is 1/21/2. Its constancy still comes from McShane’s theorem. The cusp partition and unfolding do different jobs: one sums gaps on one surface; the other integrates a curve sum over all surfaces.

How the example becomes a volume recursion.

For a surface with positive boundary lengths, Mirzakhani’s generalized McShane identity partitions a distinguished boundary of length L1L_1 by gap functions associated with embedded pairs of pants. Its right-hand side is L1L_1. Integration makes that side L1Vg,n(L)L_1V_{g,n}(L). On the other side, group the pants by topological type and unfold each orbit sum. Removing the chosen pants leaves surfaces of smaller complexity. Their volumes, together with the explicitly known gap kernels and the length–twist factors, evaluate the unfolded terms. Consequently the unknown volume is related to lower-complexity volumes; this is a recursion, not just another integral representation [75, Section 8]. The punctured-torus example is especially simple because its cut surface is already a pair of pants, whose fixed-boundary moduli space is a point.

17.2 Operations that preserve constancy

Finite linear combinations of constant series preserve constancy. Parameter integrals do too when the constant is integrable and Fubini’s theorem applies to the joint sum and integral, or when all terms are nonnegative and Tonelli’s theorem applies. For differentiation on an identified parameter family, one sufficient hypothesis is local uniform absolute convergence of both the series and its first derivatives. Uniform convergence of the original series alone is insufficient. Products give constant double series when Tonelli’s theorem or absolute convergence applies. These operations construct kernels together with their required convergence hypotheses.

Their geometric interpretation needs to be retained. A product usually indexes pairs of curves that may intersect; it is not automatically a disjoint-multicurve sum. A parameter derivative also differentiates lengths if the metric varies with that parameter. In particular, differentiating a constant series with respect to the metric gives the one-form identity ∑γF′(ℓγ)⁡dℓγ=0,\sum_\gamma F'(\ell_\gamma)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm d\ell_\gamma=0,(17.5) under the stated local C1C^1 control, for a kernel with no additional explicit metric dependence. It does not generally give another scalar identity by differentiating only the displayed weight.

Two possible tests for a proposed constant series follow. One can try to show that (17.5) vanishes on a spanning set of tangent directions at every point; a C1C^1 series is then constant on each connected component. Alternatively, for real-valued S∈L2S\in L^2 on a finite positive-volume moduli space, let S¯\overline S be its mean. Then ∫ℳ(S−S¯)2⁡dVWP=0\int_{\mathcal M}(S-\overline S)^2\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm dV_{\mathrm{WP}}=0(17.6) proves constancy almost everywhere, and everywhere if SS is continuous, since WP measure has full support. For a complex-valued series use |S−S¯|2|S-\overline S|^2 instead of the square. The second-moment calculation involves pairs of curves, including their intersection patterns. It can be substantially harder than the first unfolding integral.

Thus a useful search for further identities should ask for more than formal closure: a natural index set, a manageable positive weight, a geometric partition or telescoping mechanism, and a convergence proof. The constructions in Sections 18 and 20 illustrate these requirements.

17.3 Other established constant series

There are also constant series with substantially different kernels. For example, write ℒ(x)=Li⁡2(x)+12log⁡xlog⁡(1−x)\mathcal L(x)=\operatorname{Li}_2(x)+\tfrac12\log x\log(1-x) for the Rogers dilogarithm on (0,1)(0,1), extended continuously to its endpoints, and define D(r)=ℒ(sech⁡2(r/2))+2ℒ(1+e−r2)−2ℒ(1−e−r2).\begin{aligned} D(r)={}&\mathcal L\bigl(\mathop{\mathrm{sech}}^2(r/2)\bigr)\\ &+2\mathcal L\left(\frac{1+e^{-r}}2\right) -2\mathcal L\left(\frac{1-e^{-r}}2\right). \end{aligned} On a complete finite-area once-punctured torus, Hu–Tan’s identity is ∑α simple, unorientedD(ℓα)=π22.\sum_{\alpha\text{ simple, unoriented}}D(\ell_\alpha)=\frac{\pi^2}{2}.(17.7) Here the index set is the nonperipheral simple spectrum with its curve multiplicities [63, Theorem 2, equation (2)]. Their Theorem 1 treats a positive geodesic boundary with a different, boundary-dependent kernel; the displayed DD is the cusped specialization.

For a closed oriented surface of genus g≥2g\ge2, Luo–Tan give explicit dilogarithmic weights f(P),g(T)f(P),g(T) on properly embedded geometric pairs of pants and one-holed tori, each subsurface counted once, such that ∑Pf(P)+∑Tg(T)=8π2(g−1)=Vol⁡(T1X).\sum_P f(P)+\sum_T g(T)=8\pi^2(g-1)=\operatorname{Vol}(T^1X).(17.8) Their construction partitions unit tangent directions up to a null set [70, Theorem 1.1 and Section 1.2]. Angular measure 2π2\pi on each unit tangent circle, together with area 4π(g−1)4\pi(g-1), explains the normalization on the right. It illustrates another source of constant series: the invariant measure being partitioned need not live only on a horocycle or a geodesic boundary. The precise, longer subsurface weights are given in that reference.
