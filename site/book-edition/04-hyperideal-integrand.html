4 Hyperideal polyhedra: an actual local integrand

4.1 From vertices to orthogeodesic lengths

A strictly hyperideal vertex lies outside the closed ball in the projective model, while every edge meets the ball. Its polar plane cuts off the vertex. After all such truncations, the original faces form a bordered hyperbolic surface; the truncation polygons give its geodesic boundaries. Ideal vertices can be included by a limiting procedure and give cusps.

For a hyperideal tetrahedron choose unit spacelike representatives v1,…,v4v_1,\ldots,v_4 in Minkowski space of signature (3,1)(3,1). Choose their signs so that they represent the vertices in one projective affine chart and have negative pairwise inner products. Set qij=−⟨vi,vj⟩=cosh⁡aij>1.q_{ij}=-\langle v_i,v_j\rangle=\cosh a_{ij}>1.(4.1) Here aija_{ij} is the length of the original edge between the two polar truncation planes. It is an orthogeodesic of the intrinsic bordered surface. The six aija_{ij} specify the vertex configuration up to ambient isometry on the admissible tetrahedral domain.

Let bijkb_i^{jk} be the side of the truncation triangle at vertex ii that lies in the original face ijkijk. The right-angled hexagon cosine rule gives cosh⁡bijk=cosh⁡aijcosh⁡aik+cosh⁡ajksinh⁡aijsinh⁡aik,Li=∑{j,k}⊂{1,2,3,4}\{i}bijk.\cosh b_i^{jk}= \frac{\cosh a_{ij}\cosh a_{ik}+\cosh a_{jk}} {\sinh a_{ij}\sinh a_{ik}}, \qquad L_i=\sum_{\{j,k\}\subset\{1,2,3,4\}\setminus\{i\}}b_i^{jk}.(4.2) The four LiL_i are intrinsic boundary lengths. They are not the sums of the exterior dihedral angles around the hyperideal vertices.

These formulas also describe the domain concretely. The Gram matrix Gii=1,Gij=−qij(i≠j)G_{ii}=1,\qquad G_{ij}=-q_{ij}\quad(i\ne j)(4.3) must have signature (3,1)(3,1). With all qij>1q_{ij}>1, the strict tetrahedral condition is det⁡G<0\det G<0. Indeed, each 3×33\times3 principal determinant is 1−qij2−qik2−qjk2−2qijqikqjk<01-q_{ij}^2-q_{ik}^2-q_{jk}^2-2q_{ij}q_{ik}q_{jk}<0. The trace and a negative 2×22\times2 principal determinant then show that this block has signature (2,1)(2,1). Interlacing and det⁡G<0\det G<0 give signature (3,1)(3,1) for GG.

There is also a direct check of the geometric inequalities used below. At vertex ii, put uj=vj+qijviqij2−1(j≠i).u_j=\frac{v_j+q_{ij}v_i}{\sqrt{q_{ij}^2-1}}\quad(j\ne i). These are unit timelike vectors in vi⟂v_i^\perp, with −⟨uj,uk⟩=cosh⁡bijk>1-\langle u_j,u_k\rangle=\cosh b_i^{jk}>1, so they lie in the same sheet of its hyperboloid. Their Gram determinant is det⁡G/∏j≠i(qij2−1)<0\det G/\prod_{j\ne i}(q_{ij}^2-1)<0 by the Schur complement. Thus they are three noncollinear points in ℍ2\mathbb H^2: their pairwise distances bijkb_i^{jk} form a genuine triangle and satisfy strict triangle inequalities. Conversely a Gram realization with this signature lies in a common affine chart: a positive eigenvector for the largest eigenvalue of I−GI-G gives a timelike positive combination of the viv_i whose inner products with all four are negative. Every projective edge meets the ball because ⟨vi+vj,vi+vj⟩=2−2qij<0\langle v_i+v_j,v_i+v_j\rangle=2-2q_{ij}<0.

It is possible to glue right-angled hexagons from a wider set of positive arc-length assignments and obtain a valid intrinsic bordered surface. Such arcs need not be the edges of its convex hyperideal realization. Thus the polyhedral domain must be imposed when the purpose is to integrate a canonical polyhedral cell.

4.2 The WP density on a fixed-boundary leaf

Mondello’s Poisson formula for arc lengths has the boundary kernel KL(s)=sinh⁡(L/2−s)sinh⁡(L/2),0<s<L.K_L(s)=\frac{\sinh(L/2-s)}{\sinh(L/2)},\qquad 0<s<L.(4.4) For two different arcs e,fe,f, the bracket in convention (1.1) is one half the sum of these kernels over their common boundary endpoints, with oriented boundary distances from the endpoint of ee to that of ff. Changing the order changes the sign, since KL(L−s)=−KL(s)K_L(L-s)=-K_L(s). The factor 1/21/2 results from evaluating the ordered wedge sum in [79, Theorem 4.1]. That theorem is stated for a compact surface with strictly positive geodesic boundaries and a maximal disjoint orthogeodesic arc system. Boundary lengths are Casimirs of the Poisson tensor; inversion is performed only after restricting to a fixed-boundary leaf. The matrix in all six tetrahedral arc lengths is therefore not the matrix to invert.

Restrict now to the two-dimensional leaf where L1,…,L4L_1,\ldots,L_4 are fixed. The arcs a12,a13a_{12},a_{13} share just the boundary at vertex 11, and the relevant distance is b=b123b=b_1^{23}, up to orientation. Inverting the two-dimensional Poisson tensor gives ⁡dVWP=2sinh⁡(L1/2)sinh⁡(L1/2−b123)|da12∧da13|.\boxed{ \mathop{}\!\mathrm dV_{\mathrm{WP}}= 2\frac{\sinh(L_1/2)}{\sinh(L_1/2-b_1^{23})} \left|\mathop{}\!\mathrm da_{12}\wedge\mathop{}\!\mathrm da_{13}\right|.}(4.5) Since the three truncation sides form a genuine triangle, b123<L1/2b_1^{23}<L_1/2. Thus the denominator is positive, the bracket is nonzero, and these two arc lengths are local coordinates on this leaf.

Formula (4.5) is an actual expression for the volume integrand in terms of the hyperideal geometry. Its remaining variables are functions on the fixed-LL leaf; they cannot be held independently fixed while a12,a13a_{12},a_{13} vary.

For an expression directly in the vertex Gram entries, put D=q122+q132+q232+2q12q13q23−1.D=q_{12}^2+q_{13}^2+q_{23}^2+2q_{12}q_{13}q_{23}-1. Equation (4.2) gives cosh⁡b=q12q13+q23q122−1q132−1,sinh⁡b=Dq122−1q132−1.\cosh b=\frac{q_{12}q_{13}+q_{23}} {\sqrt{q_{12}^2-1}\sqrt{q_{13}^2-1}},\qquad \sinh b=\frac{\sqrt D} {\sqrt{q_{12}^2-1}\sqrt{q_{13}^2-1}}. Substituting in (4.5) and using ⁡dqij=sinh⁡aij⁡daij\mathop{}\!\mathrm dq_{ij}=\sinh a_{ij}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm da_{ij} yields ⁡dVWP=2|dq12∧dq13|q12q13+q23−coth⁡(L1/2)D.\boxed{ \mathop{}\!\mathrm dV_{\mathrm{WP}}= \frac{2\left|\mathop{}\!\mathrm dq_{12}\wedge\mathop{}\!\mathrm dq_{13}\right|} {q_{12}q_{13}+q_{23}-\coth(L_1/2)\sqrt D}.}(4.6) The positivity follows from the same truncation-triangle inequality. For a concrete normalization check, take qij=2q_{ij}=2 for every edge. Then G=3I−2𝟏𝟏tG=3I-2\mathbf1\mathbf1^t has determinant −135-135, aij=bijk=a=arcosh⁡2a_{ij}=b_i^{jk}=a=\operatorname{arcosh}2, and Li=3aL_i=3a. The kernel is K3a(a)=1/5K_{3a}(a)=1/5, so the bracket is 1/101/10 and (4.5) has coefficient 1010. In Gram coordinates D=27D=27 and coth⁡(3a/2)=33/5\coth(3a/2)=3\sqrt3/5, giving coefficient 10/310/3 in (4.6), exactly as sinh⁡2a=3\sinh^2a=3 requires. This evaluates a local density at one admissible point; it does not solve the fixed-boundary equations throughout a cell.

The cusp limit requires renormalization. As a positive boundary shrinks, the raw distances to its truncation plane diverge; subtract the endpoint terms corresponding to a chosen limiting horocycle before comparing with lambda lengths. If s/L→ρs/L\to\rho, the kernel itself has the regular limit KL(s)→1−2ρK_L(s)\to1-2\rho. A fixed-perimeter horocycle gauge is different from the four-unit-lambda gauge in (3.2); changing that gauge changes coordinate Jacobians, while preserving the descended WP form. This is the cuspidal limiting procedure underlying [79, Theorem 4.5].

4.3 General cells and what remains to integrate

The same procedure applies to any arc system. On a fixed-boundary leaf, choose independent arc coordinates x1,…,x2dx_1,\ldots,x_{2d} and form the antisymmetric matrix Bef={xe,xf}WP.B_{ef}=\{x_e,x_f\}_{\mathrm{WP}}. If it is nonsingular, then ⁡dVWP=|⁡dx1⋯⁡dx2d||Pf⁡B|=|⁡dx1⋯⁡dx2d|det⁡B.\boxed{ \mathop{}\!\mathrm dV_{\mathrm{WP}}=\frac{|\mathop{}\!\mathrm dx_1\cdots\mathop{}\!\mathrm dx_{2d}|}{|\mathop{\mathrm{Pf}}B|} =\frac{|\mathop{}\!\mathrm dx_1\cdots\mathop{}\!\mathrm dx_{2d}|}{\sqrt{\det B}}.}(4.7) This is simply the top exterior power of the inverse Poisson matrix. It turns a local arc-bracket formula into a local volume formula in every dimension.

Bao–Bonahon’s angle characterization assumes a planar 33-connected dual graph, 0<θe<π0<\theta_e<\pi, circuit sums at least 2π2\pi with equality only around ideal vertices, and sums greater than π\pi on embedded paths joining distinct vertices of one dual face without lying in its boundary [46, Theorems 1–2]. The path condition must not be dropped when passing from ideal to hyperideal cells. For a complete genus-zero hyperbolic metric with finitely many cusps and infinite-area ends, the global realization theorem gives a unique finite-vertex convex generalized hyperbolic polyhedron, up to congruence. This is the case without finite cone singularities of the precise restatement in [58, Theorem 1.1]; Section 1.3 there attributes its proof to Schlenker [93]. For a bordered surface, one attaches funnels and then truncates along the polar planes. Rivin’s intrinsic theorem supplies ideal existence and generalized uniqueness [87, Theorems 1.1–1.3]. The local Gram and Poisson calculations above are independent of invoking this global existence theorem.

For fixed LiL_i, however, the angle cell is cut out further by the nonlinear equations (4.2). The local density is therefore more complicated than in the ideal case. The known total V0,4(L1,L2,L3,L4)=2π2+12∑iLi2V_{0,4}(L_1,L_2,L_3,L_4)=2\pi^2+\tfrac12\sum_iL_i^2 provides a stringent target for a direct evaluation of these cell integrals. No such full hyperideal evaluation is carried out in this monograph.

Budd’s metric-map polynomials obey, for g=0,1g=0,1, Vg,n(2π)(α1,…,αn)=22−2g−nVg,n(α12−4π2,…,αn2−4π2).V_{g,n}^{(2\pi)}(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n) =2^{2-2g-n}V_{g,n}\bigl(\sqrt{\alpha_1^2-4\pi^2},\ldots, \sqrt{\alpha_n^2-4\pi^2}\bigr).(4.8) This does not extend to g≥2g\ge2. Nor does the displayed substitution describe the actual relation between hyperideal angle perimeters and truncation boundary lengths [48, Corollary 2 and Section 1.3]. It is useful evidence of a connection, with a separate geometric question behind it.

4.4 A further tree-coordinate result

There is now an additional concrete integration route for genus zero with a distinguished cusp. Budd–Meeusen–Zonneveld construct a spine bijection from a full-measure subset to explicitly decorated tree polytopes [49, Theorem 1]. Writing mm for the total number of ends, their statement has the form ℳ0,m∘(0,𝑳)↔⨆𝔱𝒜𝔱(𝑳),⁡dVWP=2m−3⁡d2m−6u𝔱.\mathcal M_{0,m}^{\circ}(0,\mathbf L)\ \longleftrightarrow\ \bigsqcup_{\mathfrak t}\mathcal A_{\mathfrak t}(\mathbf L), \qquad \mathop{}\!\mathrm dV_{\mathrm{WP}}=2^{m-3}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm d^{\,2m-6}u_{\mathfrak t}.(4.9) Thus its volume is a finite sum of ordinary polytope volumes, with this specified constant density. Their construction uses spine and tree coordinates rather than the Bao–Bonahon hyperideal angle cells. It requires an origin cusp. In (4.9), u𝔱u_{\mathfrak t} means the independent coordinates used in [49, equation (5)]: two angles at each trivalent inner vertex and all but the last entry in each of the two boundary simplices of size Li/2L_i/2. It is not Euclidean hypersurface measure on the redundant coordinate space. With m=n+1m=n+1 in that paper, its factor 2n−22^{n-2} is precisely 2m−32^{m-3} here.

Zonneveld’s sequel removes that restriction using decompositions into half-tight cylinders [99, Theorem 3]. For positive boundary lengths, with a selected pair satisfying L1<L2L_1<L_2, it gives a full-measure bijection to tree and double-tree cells and identifies their WP measures. The latter cells include a gluing length and a twist parameter. The resulting finite integrations compute the general genus-zero volume polynomials; equal-length volume values follow by continuity. These are further direct combinatorial integrations, but they do not identify the spine cells with the hyperideal dihedral-angle cells. The direct evaluation in those polyhedral coordinates remains a distinct calculation proposed here.
