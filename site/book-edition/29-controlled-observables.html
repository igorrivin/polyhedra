29 From simple-curve coefficients to controlled observables

The preceding section supplies an explicit analytic norm for its mixing estimate. We now connect that norm to a selected family of simple curves and their coefficient distributions. The genus-one norm-ball obstruction first fixes the attainable scale of sharp remainders. Primitive pole-to-pole arcs then give geometric coefficient observables with uniform rotation and terminal-derivative bounds. Exact affine Gaussian calculations provide independent tests of the analytic estimates. Throughout, a fixed-surface sharp count, a moduli-averaged covariance, and the conditioning of coefficient extraction remain separate problems.

29.1 The norm-ball obstruction and the correct error scale

The genus-one length norm gives a necessary benchmark for any passage from smooth return estimates to sharp simple-curve counting. Let XX be a fixed complete hyperbolic once-punctured torus, and let BXB_X be the unit ball of the McShane–Rivin length norm on H1(X;ℝ)≃ℝ2H_1(X;\mathbb R)\simeq\mathbb R^2. Put AX(L)=#(LBX∩ℤ2),PX(L)=#(LBX∩ℤprim2).A_X(L)=\#(LB_X\cap\mathbb Z^2),\qquad P_X(L)=\#(LB_X\cap\mathbb Z^2_{\mathrm{prim}}). The first count includes the origin and nonprimitive classes. The second counts oriented primitive classes; the count of unoriented simple closed geodesics is NX(L)=12PX(L)N_X(L)=\tfrac12P_X(L). Elementary planar lattice counting and Möbius inversion give AX(L)=Area⁡(BX)L2+OX(L),(29.1)PX(L)=Area⁡(BX)ζ(2)L2+OX(Llog⁡L).(29.2)\begin{aligned} A_X(L)&=\operatorname{Area}(B_X)L^2+O_X(L),\\ P_X(L)&=\frac{\operatorname{Area}(B_X)}{\zeta(2)}L^2 +O_X(L\log L). \end{aligned} Indeed, PX(L)=∑dμ(d)(AX(L/d)−1)P_X(L)=\sum_d\mu(d)(A_X(L/d)-1), with d≪XLd\ll_X L; the boundary errors sum as L∑d≪XLd−1L\sum_{d\ll_X L}d^{-1}. These are the upper estimates behind [16, Theorem 4.1], with the orientation factor made explicit here.

The next theorem in that paper concerns a dense set of hyperbolic structures. Its mechanism is an infinitely flat boundary point whose supporting normal is rational with respect to the homology lattice. The subsequent Conjecture 4.3 proposes an O(L1/2+ε)O(L^{1/2+\varepsilon}) error for the modular torus; thus the obstruction is not asserted there for every individual surface. The phrase “at least O(L)O(L)” in Theorem 4.2 should be separated from the literal modern assertion limsup⁡|EX(L)|/L>0\limsup|E_X(L)|/L>0. The elementary argument below proves the precise consequence needed here: no fixed improvement of the exponent below one is possible under the stated flat-point hypothesis. We do not use it to claim a literal Ω(L)\Omega(L) bound.

The distinction between a fixed-surface estimate, an estimate uniform over moduli, and a smoothed estimate must be retained.

29.2 The Fourier and spectral meaning of the obstruction

The flatness obstruction has a direct oscillatory-integral interpretation. For illustration, suppose a localized smooth boundary arc has graph y=h−f(x),f(x)=axk+O(xk+1),a>0,y=h-f(x),\qquad f(x)=a x^k+O(x^{k+1}),\quad a>0, where k≥2k\geq2 is even. Apply smooth cutoffs supported near this arc and equal to one at the supporting point. Integrating the localized indicator in the normal coordinate first gives, up to rapidly decreasing terms, −e−2πirh2πir∫χ(x)e2πirf(x)⁡dx.-\frac{e^{-2\pi i r h}}{2\pi i r} \int \chi(x)e^{2\pi i r f(x)}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm dx. The substitution x=r−1/kux=r^{-1/k}u in the stationary-phase calculation gives χ0𝟏B̂(rν)=ca,ke−2πirhr−1−1/k+o(r−1−1/k),ca,k≠0.\widehat{\chi_0\mathbf1_B}(r\nu) =c_{a,k}\,e^{-2\pi i r h}r^{-1-1/k} +o(r^{-1-1/k}),\qquad c_{a,k}\ne0.(29.4) where ν\nu is the chosen normal direction and χ0\chi_0 is the localized cutoff. The familiar nondegenerate exponent is r−3/2r^{-3/2} when k=2k=2; larger contact order weakens it toward r−1r^{-1}. Localization matters: the contributions of different supporting points can interfere in the Fourier transform of the whole ball. No global lower bound follows from discarding those phases.

Under dilation, 𝟏LB̂(m)=L2𝟏B̂(Lm).\widehat{\mathbf1_{LB}}(m)=L^2\widehat{\mathbf1_B}(Lm). Thus a finite-type flat point with a lattice-rational normal contributes on the scale L1−1/kL^{1-1/k} to the corresponding localized Poisson terms. For an infinitely flat point there is no fixed finite type to insert in this formula. Proposition 29.1 supplies a direct counting argument in that case, without assuming smooth boundary regularity at all nearby points or invoking a formal stationary-phase expansion there.

There is also an exact elementary spectral model. On an auxiliary flat torus ℝ2/ℤ2\mathbb R^2/\mathbb Z^2, define the positive self-adjoint Fourier multiplier 𝖯Xe2πi⟨m,x⟩=∥m∥Xe2πi⟨m,x⟩.\mathsf P_X e^{2\pi i\langle m,x\rangle} =\|m\|_X e^{2\pi i\langle m,x\rangle}. Norm equivalence gives a discrete spectrum tending to infinity. Its counting function is AX(L)A_X(L), and restriction to the closed span of primitive Fourier modes has counting function PX(L)P_X(L). Restricting further to the even part identifies mm and −m-m and gives NX(L)N_X(L). This multiplier is defined spectrally; the nonsmooth length norm is not being asserted to be a classical smooth pseudodifferential symbol. At a differentiable point pp of its unit ball, a supporting covector ν\nu satisfies ∇∥⋅∥X(p)=ν⟨ν,p⟩.\nabla\|\cdot\|_X(p)=\frac{\nu}{\langle\nu,p\rangle}. Thus a lattice-rational normal is exactly a rational direction for the translation velocity of the associated Hamiltonian. This explains the periodic-phase interpretation, without assuming that a global smooth trace formula applies to the length norm.

The norm-ball discrepancy is therefore a spectral-projector remainder for this particular length multiplier. Its flat normal directions produce degenerate phases in a regularized Poisson or trace calculation. This is distinct from complementary-series spectrum for the SL2(ℝ)SL_2(\mathbb R) action on the marked-torus moduli space. In particular, it does not contradict the temperedness statement or the smooth correlation estimate in Section 28. The rotation-Sobolev issue depends on what is held fixed. A sharp indicator of a nonradial region in fixed physical vector coordinates generally has rotational jumps, so smoothing it introduces growing derivative norms. By contrast, hyperbolic length is unchanged when the flat charts are rotated while the underlying conformal surface stays fixed. A sharp cutoff in that invariant length can therefore lie in the rotation generator’s domain, and a radial flat cutoff can be rotation invariant as well. Sharpness alone is not an obstruction to the stated mixing bound. For those invariant observables, the remaining boundary and regularity issues enter when taking flow derivatives or passing from an averaged correlation to a fixed-surface counting remainder.

29.3 A coefficient observable from primitive pole-to-pole arcs

The marked-torus construction supplies an explicit family of simple curves for which the coefficient data become observables with controlled norms. This family carries a flat-length cutoff as well as a hyperbolic-length weight. The two length parameters remain distinct.

Work on the finite labeled model Y2Y_2 of Section 28.5. Write E=ℝ2/gℤ2E=\mathbb R^2/g\mathbb Z^2, with its origin one of the four ramification points of the pillowcase map. The preimage of an embedded arc joining two branch values of a double cover is a circle. Choose an even f∈Cc2(ℝ2)f\in C_c^2(\mathbb R^2) supported in the unit disk, a bounded Borel function h:(0,∞)→ℂh:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb C, and parameters R≥1R\geq1, L>0L>0. 29.7 Affine displacement sums: an exact isometric model

The homogeneous calculation can be tested on explicit geometric sums. These are the relative-period Siegel–Veech transforms discussed in [108, Section 6.1]. By polarization and equivariance, ∫Y2Ff,j(x)¯Fh,j(atx)⁡dμ(x)=∫ℝ2f(x)¯h(atx)⁡dx.\boxed{ \int_{Y_2}\overline{F_{f,j}(x)}F_{h,j}(a_tx)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm d\mu(x) =\int_{\mathbb R^2}\overline{f(x)}h(a_tx)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm dx. }(29.16) Indeed the Fourier-side integral is ∫f̂(ξ)¯ĥ(a−tξ)⁡dξ\int\overline{\widehat f(\xi)}\widehat h(a_{-t}\xi)\mathop{}\!\mathrm d\xi. Schwartz domination justifies the limit.

29.8 Two marks: the rank-one divergence and its removal

The raw product Ff1,1Ff2,2F_{f_1,1}F_{f_2,2} need not be in L2L^2, even for smooth rapidly decreasing tests. Its conditional squared norm is A1(g)A2(g),Aj(g)=∑n≠0|f̂j(g−Tn)|2.A_1(g)A_2(g),\qquad A_j(g)=\sum_{n\ne0}|\widehat f_j(g^{-T}n)|^2. Write the collinear frequency pairs as (an,bn)(an,bn), where nn is primitive modulo sign and a,b∈ℤ\{0}a,b\in\mathbb Z\setminus\{0\}. Primitive Siegel unfolding gives the rank-one contribution 12ζ(2)∑a,b≠0∫ℝ2e−2π(a2+b2)|x|2⁡dx=14ζ(2)∑a,b≠01a2+b2=∞.\frac1{2\zeta(2)}\sum_{a,b\ne0} \int_{\mathbb R^2}e^{-2\pi(a^2+b^2)|x|^2}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm dx =\frac1{4\zeta(2)}\sum_{a,b\ne0}\frac1{a^2+b^2} =\infty. The divergence is logarithmic. More generally, if both ∫fj\int f_j are nonzero, Fourier continuity gives Aj(g)≫ε−1A_j(g)\gg\varepsilon^{-1} when the dual lattice has a primitive vector of length ε≪1\varepsilon\ll1; the cusp measure is comparable to ε⁡dε\varepsilon\mathop{}\!\mathrm d\varepsilon. Centering has removed zero frequencies but leaves precisely this collinear-frequency obstruction.

There is an explicit L2L^2 replacement. Retain only the full-rank frequency matrices: Hf1,f2(2)(g,u1,u2)=∑n1,n2∈ℤ2det⁡(n1,n2)≠0f̂1(g−Tn1)f̂2(g−Tn2)e2πi(n1⋅u1+n2⋅u2).H_{f_1,f_2}^{(2)}(g,u_1,u_2) =\sum_{\substack{n_1,n_2\in\mathbb Z^2\\\det(n_1,n_2)\ne0}} \widehat f_1(g^{-T}n_1)\widehat f_2(g^{-T}n_2) e^{2\pi i(n_1\cdot u_1+n_2\cdot u_2)}.(29.18) The series is absolutely convergent on each fixed torus fiber. The rank restriction is invariant under the modular transition and under the SL2SL_2 action. It is a fiber Fourier projection, not a positivity-preserving deletion of physical segments. It is defined fiberwise before global integrability has been proved; no orthogonal projection on the global L2L^2 space is being applied to the raw product.

29.9 An exact Gaussian two-mark correlation

Take f1=f2=f=e−π|x|2f_1=f_2=f=e^{-\pi|x|^2} and write H(2)=Hf,f(2)H^{(2)}=H_{f,f}^{(2)}. The identity ∑k≥1σ1(k)kqk=−∑n≥1log⁡(1−qn),0<q<1,\sum_{k\geq1}\frac{\sigma_1(k)}k q^k =-\sum_{n\geq1}\log(1-q^n),\qquad 0<q<1, also expresses the answer by the ordinary partition Euler product. Floating-point evaluation of this bound is not an interval enclosure of the total computational error, and checking finitely many times is not a proof of the general mixing theorem.

29.10 Sharp boundaries and the rotation norm

The exact isometry also displays a regularity cost of sharp cutoffs. For a bounded convex body B⊂ℝ2B\subset\mathbb R^2, ∥F𝟏B,j∘kθ−F𝟏B,j∥22=area⁡(B△kθB).\|F_{\mathbf1_B,j}\circ k_\theta-F_{\mathbf1_B,j}\|_2^2 =\operatorname{area}(B\mathbin\triangle k_\theta B). The first variation of area under rotation, interpreted using the almost-everywhere outer normal of a convex boundary, gives area⁡(B△kθB)=|θ|∫∂B|Jx⋅n(x)|⁡ds+o(|θ|).\operatorname{area}(B\mathbin\triangle k_\theta B) =|\theta|\int_{\partial B}|Jx\cdot n(x)|\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm ds+o(|\theta|). Unless BB is rotationally invariant, this rules out a finite L2L^2 rotation derivative. Equivalently, the sharp transform has angular Sobolev regularity of order s<1/2s<1/2, but not s≥1/2s\geq1/2. The direct summation of tempered KK-type estimates requires an order greater than 1/21/2; the earlier theorem uses order one.

This is a generic boundary-regularity phenomenon. It is not by itself the more specific arithmetic obstruction from the unusually flat McShane–Rivin norm ball. It explains why a smooth correlation bound cannot be applied to a sharp norm-ball count while leaving the observable norm unchanged. Here the body BB is held fixed in physical Euclidean coordinates. A cutoff depending only on the hyperbolic length ℓX(α)\ell_X(\alpha) is unchanged when the quadratic differential is rotated over the same conformal surface XX, and does not incur this particular rotation loss. These two kinds of cutoffs must be distinguished. The fixed-surface primitive-lattice count, its Möbius inversion, and its unsmoothing error remain distinct from the affine mean-square identities proved here.
