2 Ideal polyhedra, angle cells, and a partition function

The aim is to replace an integral over surfaces by ordinary integrals over finitely many regions of angle assignments. Two issues have to be settled: which assignments come from polyhedra, and what density they carry. An ideal vertex lies on the sphere at infinity of hyperbolic three-space. It has no finite distance from an interior point, but a polyhedron with ideal vertices can have finite volume. Its boundary metric has a cusp at each vertex.

2.1 The geometric parametrization

In the polyhedral cell discussion take n≥4n\ge4; the three-ended sphere is the zero-dimensional pants base case.

The dual graph has a vertex for each face of the polyhedron and an edge crossing each original edge. A dual face encircles one original vertex. The orientation supplies a cyclic order of edges at every dual vertex; a graph with these cyclic orders is a ribbon graph. Thickening its edges to narrow strips recovers an oriented surface with boundary. Here the complementary faces are labeled by the punctures. A simple circuit is a closed edge path with no repeated vertices except its starting/ending vertex.

Rivin’s intrinsic realization theorem identifies a complete finite-area hyperbolic metric on an nn-punctured sphere with the boundary metric of a convex ideal polyhedron, uniquely up to ambient isometry. Flat doubles of ideal polygons occur as limiting configurations [87]. For oriented moduli one retains the orientation of the boundary and uses orientation-preserving equivalences.

Fix a combinatorial polyhedron and write θe\theta_e for its exterior dihedral angles. In the dual graph, the ideal conditions are 0<θe<π,(2.1)∑e∈∂fθe=2πfor every dual face f,(2.2)∑e∈Cθe>2πfor every simple dual circuit not bounding a face.(2.3)\begin{aligned} &0<\theta_e<\pi,\\ &\sum_{e\in\partial f}\theta_e=2\pi &&\text{for every dual face }f,\\ &\sum_{e\in C}\theta_e>2\pi &&\text{for every simple dual circuit not bounding a face.} \end{aligned} Here the dual graph is the planar, 33-connected graph of a convex polyhedron, with its oriented embedding fixed. With these hypotheses, these are Rivin’s characterization [88]. Thus the admissible angle assignments form an open convex polytope in an affine subspace. Generic polyhedra have triangular faces; their dual maps are trivalent and have 3n−63n-6 edges. The nn perimeter conditions are independent: their matrix is the unsigned vertex–edge incidence matrix of the primal triangulation. A row relation assigns numbers cvc_v with cv+cw=0c_v+c_w=0 across every edge; a triangular face forces its three numbers to vanish, and connectedness forces all of them to vanish. The dimension is therefore 2n−62n-6.

An edge of the dual graph has metric length θe\theta_e. The resulting metric map has face perimeters 2π2\pi and no short nonfacial circuit. This is the genus-zero instance of a 2π2\pi-irreducible metric map. It gives the precise combinatorial object behind the proposed sum over polyhedral types.

For example, an ideal tetrahedron has six edges and four ideal vertices. At the regular tetrahedron each interior dihedral angle is π/3\pi/3, hence each exterior angle θe\theta_e is 2π/32\pi/3. The three angles around a dual face add to 2π2\pi; a nonfacial four-edge circuit has sum 8π/3>2π8\pi/3>2\pi. The four independent linear perimeter equations leave 6−4=26-4=2 free parameters, agreeing with the real dimension of the four-punctured-sphere moduli space. These are lengths of the dual metric graph, measured in radians; they are not the infinite lengths of the original ideal edges.

2.2 A normalized cell integral

A sum over combinatorial types must avoid counting the same labeled, oriented object several times, and an integral on a constraint surface must specify its measure. The first issue produces the automorphism factor below. The second can already be seen in the toy constraint x+y=Lx+y=L with x,y>0x,y>0: ∫x,y>0f(x,y)δ(x+y−L)⁡dx⁡dy=∫0Lf(x,L−x)⁡dx.\int_{x,y>0} f(x,y)\,\delta(x+y-L)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm dx\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm dy =\int_0^L f(x,L-x)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm dx. Here δ\delta is the Dirac delta, characterized by ∫f(u)δ(u−a)⁡du=f(a)\int f(u)\delta(u-a)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm du=f(a). The measure on this line is ⁡dx\mathop{}\!\mathrm dx, whereas its Euclidean arc length is 2⁡dx\sqrt2\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm dx. Disintegration means choosing the measure on each fiber so that integration over fibers and then over the constraint value recovers the original ambient integral. This distinction is the reason for retaining the determinant in the graph formula.

For a dual ribbon graph GG with labeled faces, define its constrained Lebesgue measure by disintegrating ∏e⁡dθe\prod_e\mathop{}\!\mathrm d\theta_e with respect to the face-perimeter map. This notation specifies a disintegrated measure, rather than Euclidean hypersurface measure with an unstated coarea factor. Concretely, let PP be the perimeter matrix and split the edge coordinates as (u,v)(u,v) so that the square n×nn\times n block PvP_v is invertible. On the fiber Puu+Pvv=𝜶P_u u+P_vv=\boldsymbol\alpha, the measure is ⁡dνG=|⁡du1⋯⁡du2n−6||det⁡Pv|,v=Pv−1(𝜶−Puu).\mathop{}\!\mathrm d\nu_G=\frac{|\mathop{}\!\mathrm du_1\cdots\mathop{}\!\mathrm du_{2n-6}|}{|\det P_v|}, \qquad v=P_v^{-1}(\boldsymbol\alpha-P_u u). Thus deleting nn dependent edge variables without this determinant would change the normalization.

The ideal cell description is known to give a volume calculation, beyond the possibility suggested by the realization theorem. Charbonnier–David–Eynard compare the angle measure with the WP form through lambda lengths [50, Section 3.2]. In Budd’s normalization of metric-map volume, V0,n(0,…,0)=2n−2∑[G]1|Aut⁡(G)|∫𝒫G⁡dνG(2π,…,2π).\boxed{ V_{0,n}(0,\ldots,0)= 2^{n-2}\sum_{[G]}\frac{1}{|\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}(G)|} \int_{\mathcal P_G}\mathop{}\!\mathrm d\nu_G(2\pi,\ldots,2\pi).}(2.5) Here 𝒫G\mathcal P_G imposes positivity and irreducibility, GG ranges over oriented trivalent maps with labeled faces, and automorphisms preserve this data. Nontrivalent strata have zero volume. Budd gives this interpretation and the normalization explicitly [48, Section 1.3]. Equivalently, his rooted-map measure gives each root weight 1/(2|E(G)|)1/(2|E(G)|); summing the 2|E(G)|/|Aut⁡(G)|2|E(G)|/|\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}(G)| rooted versions gives the displayed unrooted weight. Reversing the cyclic orders is not an allowed equivalence of oriented maps.

Thus there is a genuine partition function over polyhedral combinatorial types. Formula (2.5) is a finite cellwise integral at fixed nn; it is not a sum over geodesics. The factors in it encode three choices that should never be suppressed: labels, orientation, and the normalization of the constrained measure. Section 3 checks them directly for n=4n=4.

2.3 The string-theoretic connection

Saadi–Zwiebach’s closed-string construction uses equal-perimeter polyhedral diagrams and quadratic differentials [92]. Zwiebach’s minimal-area formulation imposes a lower bound 2π2\pi on nontrivial closed curves and uses uniqueness to control the covering of moduli space [100]. These give a concrete historical counterpart to the suggestion that such cells should be used for integration.

Several identifications nevertheless require care. A quadratic differential is locally an expression q=φ(w)⁡dw2q=\varphi(w)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm dw^2 whose transformation rule makes |q||q| a well-defined flat metric away from its zeros and poles. A Jenkins–Strebel differential is a special one whose regular horizontal trajectories form cylinders of closed curves. Their boundaries make a metric ribbon graph. Conversely a suitable metric ribbon graph can be thickened by these flat cylinders into a surface. A hyperbolic polyhedron has an intrinsic hyperbolic boundary metric and also extrinsic dual data. These constructions may share the same combinatorial and length description without giving the same metric or the same conformal coordinate on moduli. Likewise, a string amplitude includes its specified integrand; a decomposition used for string integration does not automatically select the WP measure. Equation (2.5) uses an explicit measure comparison, not just the common vocabulary of polyhedra.
