9 Volume Hessians, finite elements, and computational complexity

The variational description of a polyhedron has a second interpretation: its infinitesimal deformation equation can be a discrete Dirichlet problem. This is an exact connection to finite elements and electrical networks, and it supplies the computational counterpart of the volume and symplectic constructions developed above. The companion study Duffin’s resistor networks, the volume of ideal polyhedra, and nearly-linear-time Laplacian solvers [118] develops this connection and its numerical examples. We incorporate the essential identities here and connect them to boundary response, Gaussian integration, flips, and the Casson–Rivin program.

9.1 The cotangent form and the correct deformation coordinates

A finite element calculation replaces a function by its values at mesh vertices, extending it linearly across each triangle. Its energy is then a quadratic form in finitely many numbers. An electrical network has the same form: a conductance ww on an edge with voltage difference vi−vjv_i-v_j contributes w(vi−vj)2w(v_i-v_j)^2. Our task is to identify precisely which polyhedral deformation variables produce this quadratic form. The Hessian is the matrix of second derivatives; its negative measures how rapidly volume decreases away from a maximum.

Let 𝒯\mathcal T be a finite, connected, consistently oriented simplicial triangulation of a closed disk, with interior vertices II and nonempty boundary vertex set BB. Each triangle has three distinct vertices. For a piecewise affine function vv on a geometric realization, integration over each triangle gives ∫|𝒯||∇v|2dA=∑e=ijwe(vi−vj)2=vTLv,we=12(cot⁡αe+cot⁡βe).\int_{|\mathcal T|}|\nabla v|^2\,dA =\sum_{e=ij}w_e(v_i-v_j)^2=v^TLv, \qquad w_e=\frac12(\cot\alpha_e+\cot\beta_e).(9.1) Here each undirected edge is counted once, αe,βe\alpha_e,\beta_e are its opposite angles, and the second term is omitted on a boundary edge. Here Lij=−wijL_{ij}=-w_{ij} for distinct joined vertices and Lii=∑j≠iwijL_{ii}=\sum_{j\ne i}w_{ij}; missing edges have weight zero. A barycentric function ϕi\phi_i is the affine function equal to one at vertex ii and zero at the other two vertices of a triangle. The collection of these functions lets us reconstruct the piecewise linear function from its vertex values.

To check the normalization, the off-diagonal stiffness entry on a triangle is ∫T∇ϕi⋅∇ϕjdA=−12cot⁡αk\int_T\nabla\phi_i\cdot\nabla\phi_j\,dA=-\tfrac12\cot\alpha_k for its barycentric functions. Row sums vanish, giving the displayed edge form. This is the cotangent network of the finite element and electrical interpretations [119].

The energy is nonnegative on every geometric mesh, even if some edge weights are negative. The additional condition for nonnegative interior conductances is local Delaunayness: we=sin⁡(αe+βe)2sin⁡αesin⁡βe≥0⇔αe+βe≤π.w_e=\frac{\sin(\alpha_e+\beta_e)} {2\sin\alpha_e\sin\beta_e}\ge0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \alpha_e+\beta_e\le\pi.(9.2) Grounding the boundary makes LIIL_{II} positive definite; a trianglewise proof, including zero Delaunay weights, is given below. When the relevant weights are nonnegative, solving its Dirichlet problem is solving Kirchhoff’s equations. Boundary-to-boundary edges do not enter this Dirichlet system; positivity of their weights is an additional condition if the full matrix is used in a different boundary problem.

Now prescribe the sums Δe=αe+βe≤π\Delta_e=\alpha_e+\beta_e\le\pi opposite interior edges, and prescribe the single opposite angle on each boundary edge. An angle structure assigns positive corner angles, with sum π\pi in every triangle and these prescribed edge sums. Assume this positive angle domain is nonempty. For planar realization the data also satisfy ∑e∋i(π−Δe)=2π\sum_{e\ni i}(\pi-\Delta_e)=2\pi at each interior vertex; the left-hand side is its total angle in every such angle structure. If the intended planar boundary is strictly convex, impose also 0<κb:=∑t∋bαt,b=∑e∋b(π−Δe)−π<π(b∈B).0<\kappa_b:=\sum_{t\ni b}\alpha_{t,b} =\sum_{e\ni b}(\pi-\Delta_e)-\pi<\pi \qquad(b\in B). Here Δe\Delta_e denotes the single prescribed opposite angle on a boundary edge. These boundary inequalities are needed for the convex polyhedral interpretation; the Hessian identities below already hold on the abstract positive angle domain. Positive feasibility alone does not impose convexity of the developed boundary. Orient the triangles consistently. If a corner cc is opposite the directed edge i→ji\to j, set αc(u)=αc0+ui−uj,u|B=0.\alpha_c(u)=\alpha_c^0+u_i-u_j,\qquad u|_B=0.(9.3) These are leading–trailing coordinates, not logarithmic scale factors. Triangle sums telescope; increments opposite a shared edge cancel. To prove that they parametrize the entire affine solution space, first use the edge equations to leave one signed increment per interior edge. The triangle equations are then the incidence equations of the connected dual graph of faces, of rank NT−1N_T-1. If EintE_{\mathrm{int}} is the number of interior edges and NTN_T the number of triangles, Euler’s formula gives dim⁡T𝒜=Eint−NT+1=|I|.\dim T\mathcal A=E_{\mathrm{int}}-N_T+1=|I|. The coordinate map is injective: if every corner increment vanishes, uu is equal across every edge, hence constant by connectedness, and the boundary condition makes it zero. It therefore has the required dimension. This argument uses the disk topology; on an annulus extra period directions can remain. In these coordinates the positive domain is Ω={u∈ℝI:αc0+ui−uj>0 for every corner c}.\Omega=\{u\in\mathbb R^I:\alpha_c^0+u_i-u_j>0 \text{ for every corner }c\}. It is nonempty, open, convex, and bounded. Positivity and the triangle sums imply αc<π\alpha_c<\pi; injectivity transfers this boundedness to uu.

Let Λ′(a)=−log⁡(2sin⁡a)\Lambda'(a)=-\log(2\sin a) be the Lobachevsky derivative and put 𝒱(α)=∑cΛ(αc)\mathcal V(\alpha)=\sum_c\Lambda(\alpha_c). At a geometric configuration this is the sum of the volumes of the ideal tetrahedra obtained by adjoining the vertex at infinity to the planar triangles. Rivin’s variational principle uses this concave functional to impose geometric closure [120; 37]. Existence and realization statements require their geometric hypotheses; prescribed Delaunay data and flat convex disks are treated separately in [121, Theorems 1.6 and 4.11]. The differential identities below do not assume that every point of Ω\Omega already has a global planar realization.

Proposition 9.1 (The exact finite-element Hessian). On the positive angle domain in the coordinates (9.3), ∇u𝒱=(log⁡Hi)i∈I,−Du2𝒱=2LII(α),\nabla_u\mathcal V=(\log H_i)_{i\in I},\qquad -D_u^2\mathcal V=2L_{II}(\alpha),(9.4) where HiH_i is the dilation accumulated by developing the incident triangles counterclockwise once around ii, taking each radial edge to the next. The matrix LIIL_{II} is positive definite when II is nonempty. In particular, at a geometric critical point the negative volume Hessian is twice the finite element stiffness matrix.

In counterclockwise development from ijij to ikik, the sine law gives the next radial length divided by the previous one as sin⁡αt,j/sin⁡αt,k\sin\alpha_{t,j}/\sin\alpha_{t,k}. Summing the logarithms over the star gives log⁡Hi\log H_i. Reversing the traversal inverts HiH_i. Since Λ″(a)=−cot⁡a\Lambda''(a)=-\cot a, the two corners opposite an interior edge contribute −(cot⁡αe+cot⁡βe)(dui−duj)2-(\cot\alpha_e+\cot\beta_e)(du_i-du_j)^2 to D2𝒱D^2\mathcal V. This is −2-2 times the corresponding summand of (9.1). Boundary increments vanish.

For strictness, put ci=cot⁡αic_i=\cot\alpha_i on one positive triangle. On a1+a2+a3=0a_1+a_2+a_3=0 its cotangent form is ∑iciai2=(c1+c3)a12+2c3a1a2+(c2+c3)a22.\sum_i c_i a_i^2 =(c_1+c_3)a_1^2+2c_3a_1a_2+(c_2+c_3)a_2^2. The first coefficient is c1+c3=sin⁡α2/(sin⁡α1sin⁡α3)>0c_1+c_3=\sin\alpha_2/(\sin\alpha_1\sin\alpha_3)>0, and the determinant is c1c2+c2c3+c3c1=1c_1c_2+c_2c_3+c_3c_1=1. Thus this form is positive definite on the triangle tangent space, even if one cotangent is negative. Applied to the three vertex differences, it vanishes exactly on constant vertex values. Summing over the connected triangulation shows that ker⁡L\ker L consists of constants. Grounding BB removes this kernel and proves the assertion. If II is empty, the determinant of the empty grounded matrix is understood to be one. ◻

Consequently 𝒱\mathcal V is strictly concave on Ω\Omega. An interior critical point, if it exists, is the unique maximum there and also a maximum on its closure by continuity. Its equations Hi=1H_i=1 kill dilation holonomy. The separately imposed total-angle equations kill rotational holonomy; convex boundary data and a global realization argument supply the polyhedral interpretation. Strict concavity alone does not place the maximizer in the interior.

For a true interior edge with 0<Δe<π0<\Delta_e<\pi, the weights satisfy we=sin⁡Δe2sin⁡αesin⁡βe≥cot⁡(Δe/2)>0.w_e=\frac{\sin\Delta_e}{2\sin\alpha_e\sin\beta_e} \ge\cot(\Delta_e/2)>0.(9.5) Thus positivity holds throughout the angle domain, before the angles close to form a geometric mesh. At Δe=π\Delta_e=\pi the weight is zero; at a geometric polyhedral realization such a diagonal subdivides a flat face. A zero abstract weight by itself asserts no embedding. The kernel proof also shows that these zero weights create no extra grounded zero modes. The finite element interpretation as an actual planar mesh applies at realization, whereas the Laplacian identity and positive-network interpretation already apply to the intermediate angle structures. Newton’s equation is precisely 2LII(α(u))δu=log⁡H(u).2L_{II}(\alpha(u))\,\delta u=\log H(u).(9.6) Figure 3 summarizes the local edge weight and its finite-element and volume-Hessian interpretations.

9.2 Boundary response, Schur complements, and a Gaussian factor

The subscripts II and BB mean interior and boundary vertices; LIBL_{IB}, for example, has interior rows and boundary columns. A Dirichlet problem fixes boundary voltages and asks for interior voltages with no injected current. The Neumann data are the currents needed at the boundary to maintain those voltages.

For a three-vertex path with unit conductances, fix endpoint voltages 00 and 11, and call the middle voltage xx. The energy is x2+(x−1)2=2(x−1/2)2+1/2x^2+(x-1)^2=2(x-1/2)^2+1/2. Minimization gives x=1/2x=1/2; the effective conductance between the endpoints is 1/21/2. Completing this square is the one-variable version of the matrix elimination below. Integrating exp⁡[−(x2+(x−1)2)/(2ℏ)]\exp[-(x^2+(x-1)^2)/(2\hbar)] over xx instead gives πℏe−1/(4ℏ)\sqrt{\pi\hbar}\,e^{-1/(4\hbar)}: the same effective energy, now with a Gaussian prefactor.

For the finite element network, eliminating the interior voltage gives vI=−LII−1LIBvB,𝒩=LBB−LBILII−1LIB.v_I=-L_{II}^{-1}L_{IB}v_B,\qquad \mathcal N=L_{BB}-L_{BI}L_{II}^{-1}L_{IB}.(9.7) The matrix 𝒩\mathcal N maps boundary voltages to boundary currents; it is the discrete Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator. Indeed, minimizing vTLvv^TLv at fixed vBv_B gives the first equation and the minimum vBT𝒩vBv_B^T\mathcal N v_B. It follows that 𝒩\mathcal N is positive semidefinite and that its kernel consists exactly of boundary constants. Grounding one boundary vertex, or working modulo constants, makes it positive definite. This energy statement allows signed boundary weights; the full passive resistor interpretation requires nonnegative weights.

The same elimination appears in volume response. For a smooth function with a nondegenerate local maximum at the origin, write 𝒱(x)=V0−12xTKx+O(|x|3),K>0,\mathscr V(x)=V_0-\tfrac12x^TKx+O(|x|^3),\qquad K>0, and let PxPx be selected linear observables. These are geometric boundary data only when the model supplies that identification; they are not the fixed coordinates u|B=0u|_B=0. The implicit-function theorem gives the local maximizing branch x(j)=K−1PTj+O(|j|2)x(j)=K^{-1}P^Tj+O(|j|^2). Thus perturbation by a source jj, or restriction to Px=bPx=b, gives the local expansions maxx near 0{𝒱(x)+jTPx}=V0+12jTχj+O(|j|3),χ=PK−1PT,maxPx=bx near 0𝒱(x)=V0−12bTχ−1b+O(|b|3),(9.8)\begin{aligned} \max_{x\text{ near }0}\{\mathscr V(x)+j^TPx\} &=V_0+\tfrac12j^T\chi j+O(|j|^3), &\chi&=PK^{-1}P^T,\\ \max_{\substack{Px=b\\x\text{ near }0}}\mathscr V(x) &=V_0-\tfrac12b^T\chi^{-1}b+O(|b|^3), \end{aligned} where the second formula assumes PP has full row rank, so χ>0\chi>0. The first is the response to a source; the second fixes its conjugate variable. They are related by a Legendre transform. In the planar problem K=2LIIK=2L_{II}; in the Dehn filling problem, the same algebra gives (16.7) and (16.8). This identifies an exact common operation without identifying their different spaces of boundary data.

More generally, split x=(y,z)x=(y,z) and eliminate yy. The effective quadratic form is Keff=Kzz−KzyKyy−1Kyz,det⁡K=det⁡Kyydet⁡Keff.K_{\mathrm{eff}}=K_{zz}-K_{zy}K_{yy}^{-1}K_{yz},\qquad \det K=\det K_{yy}\det K_{\mathrm{eff}}.(9.9) For ℏ>0\hbar>0, completing the square gives the exact real Gaussian identity ∫ℝdim⁡ye−12ℏ(y,z)TK(y,z)dy=(2πℏ)dim⁡y/2det⁡Kyye−zTKeffz/(2ℏ).\int_{\mathbb R^{\dim y}} e^{-\frac1{2\hbar}(y,z)^TK(y,z)}\,dy =\frac{(2\pi\hbar)^{\dim y/2}}{\sqrt{\det K_{yy}}} e^{-z^TK_{\mathrm{eff}}z/(2\hbar)}.(9.10) Thus elimination in the Dirichlet problem, reduction of the classical boundary action, and its leading Gaussian integration use the same Schur complement. An oscillatory or complex phase requires the specified contour and determinant branch, as in the later quantum calculations. The finite-dimensional identity does not supply those analytic data or identify an entire quantum invariant by itself.

There is also a combinatorial formula for the planar determinant. Let 𝔉B\mathfrak F_B be the spanning forests of the weighted graph in which each component contains exactly one boundary vertex. Then det⁡(−Du2𝒱)=2|I|∑F∈𝔉B∏e∈Fwe.\boxed{\det(-D_u^2\mathcal V) =2^{|I|}\sum_{F\in\mathfrak F_B}\prod_{e\in F}w_e.}(9.11) To prove it, write LII=CIdiag⁡(we)CITL_{II}=C_I\operatorname{diag}(w_e)C_I^T, where CIC_I is the incidence matrix with boundary rows removed. Cauchy–Binet expands its determinant over |I||I|-edge subsets. An incidence minor is ±1\pm1 exactly for a forest rooted at BB, and zero otherwise. This rooted-forest formula expresses the local Gaussian determinant in geometric conductances. It is a deduction from the Hessian identity and finite-dimensional linear algebra. The incidence-minor identity holds for real signed weights as well; in the Delaunay Dirichlet problem its contributing weights are nonnegative. Zero-weight edges contribute zero terms, and strictness of LIIL_{II} makes the total positive.

9.3 Why the nonlinear iteration needs a barrier

A barrier is a function that tends to +∞+\infty at the boundary of the admissible domain. Minimizing F−t𝒱F-t\mathcal V balances staying safely inside against increasing volume; increasing tt gradually reduces the influence of the barrier. A convex function ff is self-concordant here if |D3f[h,h,h]|≤2(D2f[h,h])3/2|D^3f[h,h,h]|\le2(D^2f[h,h])^{3/2} for every direction hh. This compares the change of the Hessian with the local scale set by the Hessian itself, which is what controls Newton steps. The norm ∥h∥H=hTHh\|h\|_H=\sqrt{h^THh} and its dual ∥g∥H−1=gTH−1g\|g\|_{H^{-1}}=\sqrt{g^TH^{-1}g} will be used below.

A fast linear solve does not by itself bound the number of Newton steps. In particular, −𝒱-\mathcal V is not uniformly self-concordant: near a vanishing angle, its entropy-like behavior makes the normalized third derivative unbounded. The companion study supplies a more useful estimate involving the barrier F(α)=−∑clog⁡sin⁡αc.F(\alpha)=-\sum_c\log\sin\alpha_c.(9.12) The coefficient csc⁡2αc\csc^2\alpha_c occurs in both D2FD^2F and D3𝒱D^3\mathcal V, but these are tensors of different order. The precise relation needed for optimization is the following inequality.

Proposition 9.2 (A barrier compatible with ideal volume). The dense implementation described below follows the specified schedule, with numerical diagnostics; it does not implement a nearly-linear solver or certify floating-point error.

9.4 Flips connect the local matrix to mapping-class geometry

A diagonal exchange has two different roles. Among triangulations of the convex hull of a fixed planar vertex set, with fixed nodal values, Rippa’s theorem says that Delaunay triangulation minimizes the piecewise-linear Dirichlet energy; in matrix terms L(𝒯D)≼L(𝒯)L(\mathcal T_D)\preccurlyeq L(\mathcal T) [125]. This is an ordering of quadratic forms for the same geometric data. It does not assert monotonicity of hyperbolic volume along an arbitrary sequence of topological flips. Figure 4 illustrates the comparison for one fixed Euclidean quadrilateral.

For the next comparison, graph distance counts elementary moves. A quasi-isometry preserves large distances up to fixed multiplicative and additive errors, with every target point within a bounded distance of its image. It does not identify individual edge lengths or local metrics. A mapping torus is formed from S×[0,1]S\times[0,1] by identifying (x,1)(x,1) with (f(x),0)(f(x),0) for a surface map ff; its monodromy is the mapping class of ff. Pseudo-Anosov classes are those with the invariant stretching and contracting measured foliations used in surface dynamics. The comparison theorems below are external inputs, with their surface and boundary hypotheses retained.

For a connected orientable finite-type marked surface admitting an ideal triangulation, with at least one marked point on every boundary curve, the graph of ideal triangulations and diagonal exchanges is instead a coarse geometric model of its mapping class group. For orientation- preserving classes and isotopies fixing the marked points individually, the action is proper and cocompact, and an orbit map is a quasi-isometry [126, Section 2.1, Lemma 2.3]. For a closed oriented surface of genus at least two, the pants graph is a different model, quasi-isometric to Teichmüller space with its WP metric. In this setting Brock compares the convex-core volume of the quasi-Fuchsian manifold with conformal ends X,YX,Y to dWP(X,Y)d_{\mathrm{WP}}(X,Y), with topology-dependent multiplicative and additive constants [127, Theorems 1.1–1.2]. For pseudo-Anosov monodromy on the same closed surface, mapping-torus volume and WP translation distance are comparable with topology-dependent multiplicative constants [128, Theorem 1.1]. The flip graph and pants graph have different metric roles.

There is a direct volume estimate when a flip sequence gives a layered ideal triangulation of a hyperbolic mapping torus with NN tetrahedra: straightening gives Vol⁡(M)≤Nv3\operatorname{Vol}(M)\le Nv_3, where v3v_3 is the volume of the regular ideal tetrahedron. This does not require the given triangulation to have positive geometric shapes; signed straightened volumes still have absolute value at most v3v_3. It connects a combinatorial decomposition length to three-dimensional volume. Comparing it further to WP geometry requires a theorem for the same surface and boundary conventions, rather than transferring the closed-surface theorem silently to a punctured surface.

9.5 The three-manifold extension and the remaining questions

The Casson–Rivin program uses the same ideal-tetrahedron volume and the same angle-sum constraints [37]. Its local Hessian is still a cotangent quadratic form. For leading–trailing edge variables yey_e, put pα=ye+ye′p_\alpha=y_e+y_{e'} for the pair of opposite tetrahedron edges carrying α\alpha, and cyclically for β,γ\beta,\gamma. Then −D2𝒱=∑t[cotαt(dpγ−dpβ)2+cotβt(dpα−dpγ)2+cotγt(dpβ−dpα)2].-D^2\mathcal V =\sum_t\left[ \cot\alpha_t(dp_\gamma-dp_\beta)^2 +\cot\beta_t(dp_\alpha-dp_\gamma)^2 +\cot\gamma_t(dp_\beta-dp_\alpha)^2\right].(9.15) This displays the Euclidean cusp-link triangle inside the second variation. But a square now involves sums of opposite-edge variables, and the assembled matrix need not have nonpositive off-diagonal entries or be diagonally dominant. Additional peripheral directions and removal of linear dependencies must also be included in a full coordinate system. The planar positive-Laplacian solver conclusion therefore does not transfer automatically to arbitrary three-manifold triangulations. The trianglewise barrier estimate does transfer, because it survives affine restriction.

For an ideal triangulation of an orientable compact three-manifold whose nonempty boundary is a union of tori, a positive angle structure critical for volume on the full angle space supplies the complete hyperbolic metric on its interior, by the Casson–Rivin theorem [37, Theorem 1.2]. The angle space has triangle sums π\pi and edge sums 2π2\pi; full criticality includes the peripheral directions. Edge variations alone do not impose completeness. A boundary maximizer need not realize the chosen triangulation by positive ideal tetrahedra. Optimization on a given angle polytope therefore does not solve the problem of finding a suitable geometric triangulation or yield a polynomial-time geometrization algorithm.

The finite element comparison also does not identify its real symmetric Hessian with the alternating WP form. The exact links established here are the volume Hessian, its inverse response, its Schur complement, and its Gaussian determinant. A continuum limit relating appropriately renormalized polyhedral functionals to a WP potential would require geometric convergence, a specified normalization, and control of second variations. This is a natural question, not a theorem asserted here. Likewise, in three dimensions it remains useful to ask for an effective bound on the smallest angle at an interior maximum in terms of the number of tetrahedra and a quantitative feasible margin. Such a bound would connect variational existence to numerical certification and complexity. These questions describe how the computational strand can extend the monograph’s variational and semiclassical program.
