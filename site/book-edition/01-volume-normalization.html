1 The volume problem and its normalization

What is being measured?

A moduli space is a space whose points are geometric objects, with objects related by the prescribed equivalence regarded as the same point. Here one point is an oriented hyperbolic surface, considered up to orientation-preserving isometry that preserves the labels of its ends. The genus gg counts handles; nn counts boundary components or cusps. A geodesic boundary is a closed curve that is straight for the intrinsic metric. A cusp is a noncompact end with finite area, modeled by a horoball modulo a translation. “Unparametrized” means that a boundary has a label and a length but no chosen starting point.

The volume of this space of surfaces is different from the area of one surface. For fixed boundary lengths, the latter is already 2π(2g−2+n)2\pi(2g-2+n) by Gauss–Bonnet. The volume problem asks how much room there is to vary the shape. The Weil–Petersson (WP) symplectic form supplies the measure for that question. A symplectic form is a closed, nondegenerate alternating two-form; on a space of real dimension 2d2d, its top exterior power ωd/d!\omega^d/d! is the associated volume form. The integer dd below is half the real dimension, and 𝑳\mathbf L abbreviates the ordered list (L1,…,Ln)(L_1,\ldots,L_n).

Let ℳg,n(L1,…,Ln)\mathcal M_{g,n}(L_1,\ldots,L_n) denote the moduli space of oriented hyperbolic surfaces of genus gg with nn labeled, unparametrized geodesic boundary components of prescribed lengths. A zero length denotes a cusp. Throughout, the curvature is −1-1, the parameters are fixed before taking a volume, and 2g−2+n>02g-2+n>0. Put d=3g−3+n,Vg,n(𝑳)=∫ℳg,n(𝑳)ωWPdd!.d=3g-3+n,\qquad V_{g,n}(\mathbf L)=\int_{\mathcal M_{g,n}(\mathbf L)} \frac{\omega_{\mathrm{WP}}^{d}}{d!}. A useful way to see the dimension is to cut the surface into pairs of pants, meaning spheres with three holes. The 3g−3+n3g-3+n interior cutting curves each contribute a length and a relative gluing displacement, or twist. These are the Fenchel–Nielsen coordinates (ℓj,τj)(\ell_j,\tau_j) on the marked space: a marking identifies the surface topologically with a fixed reference surface. Forgetting that marking takes the quotient by mapping classes, that is, orientation-preserving homeomorphisms up to isotopy. The length–twist coordinates are local coordinates on moduli, with possible finite symmetries. They do not make the whole moduli space a rectangular region.

For a pair of pants, (g,n)=(0,3)(g,n)=(0,3) and d=0d=0: the three boundary lengths determine its metric, so there is no shape parameter left. For a one-holed torus, (g,n)=(1,1)(g,n)=(1,1) and d=1d=1: cutting one curve leaves a pair of pants with boundary lengths (L,ℓ,ℓ)(L,\ell,\ell), and gluing the two equal boundaries requires the twist τ\tau.

Volumes are orbifold, or stack, volumes: generic ineffective stabilizers are included. In particular, V0,3=1,V0,4(𝑳)=2π2+12∑i=14Li2,V1,1(L)=L2+4π248.V_{0,3}=1,\qquad V_{0,4}(\mathbf L)=2\pi^2+\frac12\sum_{i=1}^4L_i^2, \qquad V_{1,1}(L)=\frac{L^2+4\pi^2}{48}.(1.2) The effective torus volume is twice the last expression. This convention matters both in unfolding and in the torus–sphere correspondence; see Do–Norbury [55] and Section 6. More generally, a generic ineffective stabilizer of order hh divides the effective volume by hh without rescaling the local two-form. Rescaling ωWP\omega_{\mathrm{WP}} by a constant cc instead multiplies a 2d2d-dimensional volume by cdc^d. These are separate normalization operations.

The orbifold convention can be understood in a finite quotient chart. If a finite symmetry group of order hh acts on a parameter region, its orbifold integral is the integral upstairs divided by hh. This includes elements that act trivially on the parameters, called ineffective symmetries. On the one-holed torus the generic elliptic involution contributes such a factor of two. Thus the stack volume at L=0L=0 is π2/12\pi^2/12, whereas the effective quotient volume is π2/6\pi^2/6. This changes the counting of objects with symmetries; it does not replace the local form ⁡dℓ∧⁡dτ\mathop{}\!\mathrm d\ell\wedge\mathop{}\!\mathrm d\tau by half that form.

The original geometric proposal has three separate ingredients:

  1. realize an intrinsic surface by a canonical convex polyhedron;

  2. describe the allowed polyhedra by cells, ideally linear polytopes;

  3. identify the pullback of ωWPd/d!\omega_{\mathrm{WP}}^{d}/d! on each cell.

The realization and the cell decomposition identify the integration domain. They do not, by themselves, identify its measure. In the ideal genus-zero case the measure comparison is particularly favorable; in the hyperideal case a useful local density is still available, but it is generally no longer constant in the natural angle variables.

There are also two different cross-ratios in the four-point problem. The cross-ratio is the complex parameter obtained by taking three ordered points on the Riemann sphere to 0,1,∞0,1,\infty by a Möbius transformation and recording the image of the fourth, with the ordering fixed. Four ideal vertices on ∂ℍ3\partial\mathbb H^3 have an ambient cross-ratio. The intrinsic metric on the polyhedral boundary has a conformal uniformization, and its four punctures have a conformal cross-ratio. The realization theorem relates the two parameter spaces, but these two numerical coordinates are not generally equal. Keeping them distinct is essential to the first explicit integral below.
