Volume Hessians, finite elements, and computational complexity

The variational description of a polyhedron has a second interpretation: its infinitesimal deformation equation can be a discrete Dirichlet problem. This is an exact connection to finite elements and electrical networks, and it supplies the computational counterpart of the volume and symplectic constructions developed above. The companion study Duffin’s resistor networks, the volume of ideal polyhedra, and nearly-linear-time Laplacian solvers  develops this connection and its numerical examples. We incorporate the essential identities here and connect them to boundary response, Gaussian integration, flips, and the Casson–Rivin program.

The cotangent form and the correct deformation coordinates

Let 𝒯\mathcal T triangulate a planar disk, with interior vertices II and boundary vertices BB. For a piecewise affine function vv on a geometric realization, integration over each triangle gives ∫|𝒯||∇v|2dA=∑e=ijwe(vi−vj)2=vTLv,we=12(cot⁡αe+cot⁡βe).\begin{equation} \int_{|\mathcal T|}|\nabla v|^2\,dA =\sum_{e=ij}w_e(v_i-v_j)^2=v^TLv, \qquad w_e=\frac12(\cot\alpha_e+\cot\beta_e). \label{eq:ipolyFEM} \end{equation} Here αe,βe\alpha_e,\beta_e are the angles opposite ee, and the second term is omitted on a boundary edge. To check the normalization, the off-diagonal stiffness entry on a triangle is ∫T∇ϕi⋅∇ϕjdA=−12cot⁡αk\int_T\nabla\phi_i\cdot\nabla\phi_j\,dA=-\tfrac12\cot\alpha_k for its barycentric functions. Row sums vanish, giving the displayed edge form. This is the cotangent network of the finite element and electrical interpretations .

The energy is nonnegative on every geometric mesh, even if some edge weights are negative. The additional condition for nonnegative interior conductances is local Delaunayness: we=sin⁡(αe+βe)2sin⁡αesin⁡βe≥0⇔αe+βe≤π.\begin{equation} w_e=\frac{\sin(\alpha_e+\beta_e)} {2\sin\alpha_e\sin\beta_e}\ge0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \alpha_e+\beta_e\le\pi. \label{eq:ipolyDelaunayweight} \end{equation} When the relevant weights are nonnegative, the grounded matrix LIIL_{II} is a positive definite graph Laplacian, and solving a Dirichlet problem is solving Kirchhoff’s equations. Boundary-to-boundary edges do not enter this Dirichlet system; positivity of their weights is an additional condition if the full matrix is used in a different boundary problem.

Now prescribe the sums Δe=αe+βe≤π\Delta_e=\alpha_e+\beta_e\le\pi opposite interior edges, and prescribe the single opposite angle on each boundary edge. An angle structure assigns positive corner angles, with sum π\pi in every triangle and these prescribed edge sums. Assume this positive angle domain is nonempty. For planar realization the data also satisfy ∑e∋i(π−Δe)=2π\sum_{e\ni i}(\pi-\Delta_e)=2\pi at each interior vertex; the left-hand side is its total angle in every such angle structure. If the intended planar boundary is strictly convex, impose also 0<κb:=∑t∋bαt,b=∑e∋b(π−Δe)−π<π(b∈B).0<\kappa_b:=\sum_{t\ni b}\alpha_{t,b} =\sum_{e\ni b}(\pi-\Delta_e)-\pi<\pi \qquad(b\in B). Here Δe\Delta_e denotes the single prescribed opposite angle on a boundary edge. These boundary inequalities are needed for the convex polyhedral interpretation; the Hessian identities below already hold on the abstract positive angle domain. Positive feasibility alone does not impose convexity of the developed boundary. Orient the triangles consistently. If a corner cc is opposite the directed edge i→ji\to j, set αc(u)=αc0+ui−uj,u|B=0.\begin{equation} \alpha_c(u)=\alpha_c^0+u_i-u_j,\qquad u|_B=0. \label{eq:ipolyAngleCoordinates} \end{equation} These are leading–trailing coordinates, not logarithmic scale factors. Triangle sums telescope; increments opposite a shared edge cancel. For a triangulated disk they parametrize the entire affine space of solutions: the map is injective, and the connected triangle–edge incidence graph together with Euler’s formula gives dimension |I||I|.

Let Λ′(a)=−log⁡(2sin⁡a)\Lambda'(a)=-\log(2\sin a) be the Lobachevsky derivative and put 𝒱(α)=∑cΛ(αc)\mathcal V(\alpha)=\sum_c\Lambda(\alpha_c). At a geometric configuration this is the sum of the volumes of the ideal tetrahedra obtained by adjoining the vertex at infinity to the planar triangles. Rivin’s variational principle uses this concave functional to impose geometric closure .

Proposition 1 (The exact finite-element Hessian). On the positive angle domain in the coordinates [eq:ipolyAngleCoordinates], ∇u𝒱=(log⁡Hi)i∈I,−Du2𝒱=2LII(α),\begin{equation} \nabla_u\mathcal V=(\log H_i)_{i\in I},\qquad -D_u^2\mathcal V=2L_{II}(\alpha), \label{eq:ipolyHessian} \end{equation} where HiH_i is the dilation accumulated by developing the incident triangles once around ii. In particular, at a geometric critical point the negative volume Hessian is twice the finite element stiffness matrix.

Proof. In a positively oriented triangle ijkijk, differentiation with respect to uiu_i gives log⁡(sin⁡αt,j/sin⁡αt,k)\log(\sin\alpha_{t,j}/\sin\alpha_{t,k}). Summing over the star of ii gives the logarithm of the product of side ratios, which is the dilation holonomy by the sine law. Since Λ″(a)=−cot⁡a\Lambda''(a)=-\cot a, the two corners opposite an interior edge contribute −(cot⁡αe+cot⁡βe)(dui−duj)2-(\cot\alpha_e+\cot\beta_e)(du_i-du_j)^2 to D2𝒱D^2\mathcal V. This is −2-2 times the corresponding summand of [eq:ipolyFEM]. Boundary increments vanish. ◻

For a true interior edge with 0<Δe<π0<\Delta_e<\pi, the weights satisfy we=sin⁡Δe2sin⁡αesin⁡βe≥cot⁡(Δe/2)>0.\begin{equation} w_e=\frac{\sin\Delta_e}{2\sin\alpha_e\sin\beta_e} \ge\cot(\Delta_e/2)>0. \label{eq:ipolyWeightLower} \end{equation} Thus positivity holds throughout the angle domain, before the angles close to form a geometric mesh. At Δe=π\Delta_e=\pi the weight is zero; such a diagonal subdivides a flat face. The finite element interpretation as an actual planar mesh applies at realization, whereas the Laplacian identity and positive-network interpretation already apply to the intermediate angle structures. Newton’s equation is precisely 2LII(α(u))δu=log⁡H(u).\begin{equation} 2L_{II}(\alpha(u))\,\delta u=\log H(u). \label{eq:ipolyNewton} \end{equation}

Boundary response, Schur complements, and a Gaussian factor

For the finite element network, eliminating the interior voltage gives vI=−LII−1LIBvB,𝒩=LBB−LBILII−1LIB.\begin{equation} v_I=-L_{II}^{-1}L_{IB}v_B,\qquad \mathcal N=L_{BB}-L_{BI}L_{II}^{-1}L_{IB}. \label{eq:ipolyDtN} \end{equation} The matrix 𝒩\mathcal N maps boundary voltages to boundary currents; it is the discrete Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator. It has the constant mode in its kernel. Grounding one boundary vertex, or working modulo constants, removes that mode.

The same elimination appears in volume response. For a nondegenerate concave maximum, write 𝒱(x)=V0−12xTKx+O(|x|3),K>0,\mathscr V(x)=V_0-\tfrac12x^TKx+O(|x|^3),\qquad K>0, and let PxPx be the selected linear boundary data. Perturbation by a source jj gives $$\begin{align} \max_x\{\mathscr V(x)+j^TPx\} &=V_0+\tfrac12j^T\chi j+O(|j|^3), &\chi&=PK^{-1}P^T,\notag\\ \max_{Px=b}\mathscr V(x) &=V_0-\tfrac12b^T\chi^{-1}b+O(|b|^3), \label{eq:ipolyBoundaryResponse} \end{align}$$ where the second formula assumes PP has full row rank. The first is the response to a source; the second fixes its conjugate boundary variable. They are related by a Legendre transform. In the planar problem K=2LIIK=2L_{II}; in the Dehn filling problem, the same algebra gives [eq:cuspHessian] and [eq:cuspmatrixHessian]. This identifies an exact common operation without identifying their different spaces of boundary data.

More generally, split x=(y,z)x=(y,z) and eliminate yy. The effective quadratic form is $$\begin{equation} K_{\rm eff}=K_{zz}-K_{zy}K_{yy}^{-1}K_{yz},\qquad \det K=\det K_{yy}\det K_{\rm eff}. \label{eq:ipolySchur} \end{equation}$$ Completing the square gives the exact real Gaussian identity $$\begin{equation} \int_{\mathbb R^{\dim y}} e^{-\frac1{2\hbar}(y,z)^TK(y,z)}\,dy =\frac{(2\pi\hbar)^{\dim y/2}}{\sqrt{\det K_{yy}}} e^{-z^TK_{\rm eff}z/(2\hbar)}. \label{eq:ipolyGaussian} \end{equation}$$ Thus elimination in the Dirichlet problem, reduction of the classical boundary action, and its leading Gaussian integration use the same Schur complement. An oscillatory or complex phase requires the specified contour and determinant branch, as in the later quantum calculations. The finite-dimensional identity does not supply those analytic data or identify an entire quantum invariant by itself.

There is also a combinatorial formula for the planar determinant. Let 𝔉B\mathfrak F_B be the spanning forests of the weighted graph in which each component contains exactly one boundary vertex. Then det⁡(−Du2𝒱)=2|I|∑F∈𝔉B∏e∈Fwe.\begin{equation} \boxed{\det(-D_u^2\mathcal V) =2^{|I|}\sum_{F\in\mathfrak F_B}\prod_{e\in F}w_e.} \label{eq:ipolyForestDeterminant} \end{equation} To prove it, write LII=CIdiag⁡(we)CITL_{II}=C_I\operatorname{diag}(w_e)C_I^T, where CIC_I is the incidence matrix with boundary rows removed. Cauchy–Binet expands its determinant over |I||I|-edge subsets. An incidence minor is ±1\pm1 exactly for a forest rooted at BB, and zero otherwise. This rooted-forest formula expresses the local Gaussian determinant in geometric conductances. It is a deduction from the Hessian identity and finite-dimensional linear algebra.

Why the nonlinear iteration needs a barrier

A fast linear solve does not by itself bound the number of Newton steps. In particular, −𝒱-\mathcal V is not uniformly self-concordant: near a vanishing angle, its entropy-like behavior makes the normalized third derivative unbounded. The companion study supplies a more useful estimate involving the barrier F(α)=−∑clog⁡sin⁡αc.\begin{equation} F(\alpha)=-\sum_c\log\sin\alpha_c. \label{eq:ipolyBarrier} \end{equation} The coefficient csc⁡2αc\csc^2\alpha_c occurs in both D2FD^2F and D3𝒱D^3\mathcal V, but these are tensors of different order. The precise relation needed for optimization is the following inequality.

Proposition 2 (A barrier compatible with ideal volume). For every positive angle structure and tangent vector hh whose coordinates sum to zero in each triangle, |D3𝒱[h,h,h]|≤3(−D2𝒱[h,h])D2F[h,h].\begin{equation} |D^3\mathcal V[h,h,h]| \le\sqrt3\,(-D^2\mathcal V[h,h])\sqrt{D^2F[h,h]}. \label{eq:ipolyCompatibility} \end{equation} Consequently F−t𝒱F-t\mathcal V is self-concordant with the standard constant for every t≥0t\ge0. The barrier FF has parameter at most the number mm of corner angles.

Proof. For one triangle set si=sin⁡αis_i=\sin\alpha_i and choose the cyclic side directions ψ=(0,α1+α2,π+α2)\psi=(0,\alpha_1+\alpha_2,\pi+\alpha_2). They satisfy ∑isieiψi=0\sum_i s_i e^{i\psi_i}=0. Every vector with h1+h2+h3=0h_1+h_2+h_3=0 can therefore be written hi=λsicos⁡φih_i=\lambda s_i\cos\varphi_i, where φi=ψi−τ\varphi_i=\psi_i-\tau. The cotangent energy identity and a trigonometric expansion give A:=−D2𝒱[h,h]=λ2s1s2s3,B:=D2F[h,h]=λ2∑icos⁡2φi,A:=-D^2\mathcal V[h,h]=\lambda^2s_1s_2s_3,\quad B:=D^2F[h,h]=\lambda^2\sum_i\cos^2\varphi_i, D3𝒱[h,h,h]=λ3s1s2s3cos⁡(φ1+φ2+φ3).D^3\mathcal V[h,h,h] =\lambda^3s_1s_2s_3\cos(\varphi_1+\varphi_2+\varphi_3). For the last identity, use cos⁡3x=(3cos⁡x+cos⁡3x)/4\cos^3 x=(3\cos x+\cos3x)/4 and the vanishing side-vector sum. Expanding the cosine of the sum shows |cos⁡(φ1+φ2+φ3)|≤∑i|cos⁡φi|≤3∑icos⁡2φi|\cos(\varphi_1+\varphi_2+\varphi_3)| \le\sum_i|\cos\varphi_i|\le\sqrt{3\sum_i\cos^2\varphi_i}. This proves the inequality on one triangle, and summation proves it globally since ∑ATBT≤(∑AT)∑BT\sum A_T\sqrt{B_T}\le(\sum A_T)\sqrt{\sum B_T}. Each −log⁡sin⁡αc-\log\sin\alpha_c is self-concordant, and, with A=−tD2𝒱[h,h]A=-tD^2\mathcal V[h,h] and B=D2F[h,h]B=D^2F[h,h], |D3(F−t𝒱)[h,h,h]|≤2B3/2+3AB≤2(A+B)3/2.|D^3(F-t\mathcal V)[h,h,h]| \le2B^{3/2}+\sqrt3 A\sqrt B\le2(A+B)^{3/2}. Finally Cauchy–Schwarz gives |DF[h]|≤mD2F[h,h]|DF[h]|\le\sqrt m\sqrt{D^2F[h,h]}, the required barrier-parameter bound. Restriction to additional affine angle constraints preserves these inequalities. ◻

For the central point α(t)\alpha(t) minimizing F−t𝒱F-t\mathcal V, the volume gap is at most m/tm/t. Indeed, concavity of sine gives DF(α)[β−α]≤mDF(\alpha)[\beta-\alpha]\le m for every feasible β\beta; combine this with stationarity and concavity of 𝒱\mathcal V. Increasing tt by a factor 1+c/m1+c/\sqrt m, with fixed sufficiently small cc, gives a bounded Newton decrement: the Hessian of F−t𝒱F-t\mathcal V dominates D2FD^2F and the barrier gradient has local norm at most m\sqrt m. Standard self-concordant Newton analysis therefore gives, starting from a centered point at t0>0t_0>0, $$\begin{equation} O\!\left(\sqrt m\log\max\left\{e,\frac{m}{t_0\varepsilon}\right\}\right) \quad\hbox{Newton steps to volume gap at most }\varepsilon. \label{eq:ipolyStepBound} \end{equation}$$ See  for the underlying path-following analysis. This is a bound for the barrier method, not for unrestricted plain Newton iteration.

In the disk coordinates the Hessian of F−t𝒱F-t\mathcal V is again a grounded planar Laplacian, now with interior conductances 2twe+csc⁡2αe+csc⁡2βe.2t w_e+\csc^2\alpha_e+\csc^2\beta_e. Nearly-linear Laplacian solvers approximate a linear solve in its energy norm . With controlled inexact-Newton tolerances, their combination with [eq:ipolyStepBound] gives the usual Õ(n3/2)\widetilde O(n^{3/2}) arithmetic-work scale after centering, up to accuracy factors, when m=O(n)m=O(n). It does not give a nearly-linear bound for the whole nonlinear realization problem. Finding a feasible point, centering it, controlling arithmetic precision, and certifying the final geometric error are separate costs. The numerical timings in the companion are experiments on selected inputs, not bounds for these additional tasks.

Flips connect the local matrix to mapping-class geometry

A diagonal exchange has two different roles. Among triangulations of the convex hull of a fixed planar vertex set, with fixed nodal values, Rippa’s theorem says that Delaunay triangulation minimizes the piecewise-linear Dirichlet energy; in matrix terms L(𝒯D)≼L(𝒯)L(\mathcal T_D)\preccurlyeq L(\mathcal T) . This is an ordering of quadratic forms for the same geometric data. It does not assert monotonicity of hyperbolic volume along an arbitrary sequence of topological flips.

For a connected orientable finite-type marked surface admitting an ideal triangulation, with at least one marked point on every boundary curve, the graph of ideal triangulations and diagonal exchanges is instead a coarse geometric model of its mapping class group. With isotopies fixing the marked points, the action is proper and cocompact, and an orbit map is a quasi-isometry . The pants graph is a different model, quasi-isometric to Teichmüller space with its WP metric, and Brock relates WP distance to convex-core volume . His mapping-torus theorem relates WP translation distance and hyperbolic volume for closed pseudo-Anosov surface bundles . These comparisons have constants depending on the surface topology; the flip graph and the pants graph should not be identified.

There is a direct volume estimate when a flip sequence gives a layered ideal triangulation of a hyperbolic mapping torus with NN tetrahedra: straightening gives Vol⁡(M)≤Nv3\operatorname{Vol}(M)\le Nv_3, where v3v_3 is the volume of the regular ideal tetrahedron. This does not require the given triangulation to have positive geometric shapes; signed straightened volumes still have absolute value at most v3v_3. It connects a combinatorial decomposition length to three-dimensional volume. Comparing it further to WP geometry requires a theorem for the same surface and boundary conventions, rather than transferring the closed-surface theorem silently to a punctured surface.

The three-manifold extension and the remaining questions

The Casson–Rivin program uses the same ideal-tetrahedron volume and the same angle-sum constraints . Its local Hessian is still a cotangent quadratic form. For leading–trailing edge variables yey_e, put pα=ye+ye′p_\alpha=y_e+y_{e'} for the pair of opposite tetrahedron edges carrying α\alpha, and cyclically for β,γ\beta,\gamma. Then −D2𝒱=∑t[cotαt(dpγ−dpβ)2+cotβt(dpα−dpγ)2+cotγt(dpβ−dpα)2].\begin{equation} -D^2\mathcal V =\sum_t\left[ \cot\alpha_t(dp_\gamma-dp_\beta)^2 +\cot\beta_t(dp_\alpha-dp_\gamma)^2 +\cot\gamma_t(dp_\beta-dp_\alpha)^2\right]. \label{eq:ipolyThreeDimensionalHessian} \end{equation} This displays the Euclidean cusp-link triangle inside the second variation. But a square now involves sums of opposite-edge variables, and the assembled matrix need not have nonpositive off-diagonal entries or be diagonally dominant. Additional peripheral directions and removal of linear dependencies must also be included in a full coordinate system. The planar positive-Laplacian solver conclusion therefore does not transfer automatically to arbitrary three-manifold triangulations. The trianglewise barrier estimate does transfer, because it survives affine restriction.

An interior critical point has the geometric interpretation supplied by the Casson–Rivin theorem. A boundary maximizer need not realize the chosen triangulation by positive ideal tetrahedra. Optimization on a given angle polytope therefore does not solve the problem of finding a suitable geometric triangulation or yield a polynomial-time geometrization algorithm.

The finite element comparison also does not identify its real symmetric Hessian with the alternating WP form. The exact links established here are the volume Hessian, its inverse response, its Schur complement, and its Gaussian determinant. A continuum limit relating appropriately renormalized polyhedral functionals to a WP potential would require geometric convergence, a specified normalization, and control of second variations. This is a natural question, not a theorem asserted here. Likewise, in three dimensions it remains useful to ask for an effective bound on the smallest angle at an interior maximum in terms of the number of tetrahedra and a quantitative feasible margin. Such a bound would connect variational existence to numerical certification and complexity. These questions describe how the computational strand can extend the monograph’s variational and semiclassical program.
